\begin{abstract}
We introduce PhAIL (Physical AI Leaderboard), a real-robot benchmark and evaluation methodology for vision-language-action (VLA) policies. Most published VLA evaluations report binary success rate, a reliability proxy that does not capture throughput; reporting them as separate scalars exposes the deeper problem that no single scalar uniquely encodes operational priority. We adopt the time-to-success CDF as a primitive that covers both axes jointly, with hard failures (drops outside the workspace, safety stops) absorbed as $T=\infty$ ghost events. Standard scalars (UPH, success rate at $\tau$, MTBF/A, completion fraction) are projections of this CDF. Each task's CDF is anchored cell-wise to a teleoperated-human reference, yielding macro-averaged \emph{Human-Relative Throughput} (HRT) as a dimensionless headline scalar. We then separate the two distinct jobs that a methodology must do: \emph{reporting} (a scalar with bootstrap intervals) and \emph{ranking} (a paired CDF-level test). On four frontier VLAs (OpenPI $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{pi05}, GR00T N1.6~\cite{groot_n1}, ACT~\cite{act}, SmolVLA~\cite{smolvla}) evaluated on a Franka FR3 across four objects at $\sim$30 paired rollouts per (model, object) cell, the top two inference models are pairwise indistinguishable on every metric tested; the third is separable; the fourth clearly worst. Subsampling shows that a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test on the macro CDF detects ranking differences on borderline pairs at $N{=}20$--$30$ paired rollouts where binary success-rate-at-threshold and the integrated RMST scalar cannot detect them within budget. The choice of test, not just the choice of $N$, determines what is resolvable. The dominant practice -- single scalars at $N{\le}25$ per condition without confidence intervals or paired tests -- is two orders of magnitude under-budgeted for the ranking claims it publishes. All policies were fine-tuned with their default open-source recipes; the contribution is methodological. Benchmark, dataset, and code: \url{https://phail.ai}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%===============================================================================

How do you tell whether one robot policy is better than another at a real task on real hardware? Most published VLA evaluations answer this with binary success rate at a fixed timeout. We argue that this answer is structurally insufficient, that the time-to-success CDF is the right primitive instead, and that once one adopts that primitive the methodology splits into two distinct jobs -- \emph{reporting} (a scalar projection of the CDF) and \emph{ranking} (a paired test on the CDF itself) -- which most current practice conflates and which behave very differently as a function of sample size.

\paragraph{Success rate is a reliability metric without a throughput axis.} Reported as ``did the policy complete the task within $\tau$ seconds?'', it is a usable proxy for reliability: with $\tau$ generous enough that an in-distribution policy succeeds, the metric tells you whether the policy is reliable. But reliability is one operational property and throughput is a different one. A 90\% success rate at $\tau{=}30$ seconds is a deployable policy; the same 90\% at $\tau{=}300$ seconds is not. The standard fix is to add a throughput metric (UPH, cycle time, completion fraction) alongside the success rate. That works, but it surfaces the deeper question: when the two scalars rank the policies differently, which do you publish? The answer is task-dependent and not interchangeable, and the choice is rarely disclosed.

\paragraph{Time-to-success $T$ covers both axes.} Let $T$ be the wall-clock time the policy needs to complete one operation of the task. A short $T$ is fast \emph{and} reliable; a long $T$ is slow; an undefined $T$ (item lost outside the workspace, safety stop fired) is a hard failure that can never recover. We extend the standard time-to-event apparatus by absorbing such hard failures as $T = \infty$ ghost events: an operation that physically cannot complete contributes one $(T, E) = (\infty, 1)$ observation rather than another censored tail. The resulting CDF $F(t) = P(T \le t)$ jointly carries reliability (its asymptote below 1, equal to the hard-failure rate) and throughput (the median of $T$ where $F$ crosses $0.5$, and $\text{UPH} \propto 1/E[T \mid T < \infty]$). Figure~\ref{fig:hero} shows the four frontier VLAs and the teleop reference under this primitive. UPH, success rate at $\tau$, MTBF/A, and completion fraction are all projections of $F$. (Our task happens to consist of many operations per episode, which we exploit for sample size; the framework applies just as well to single-operation episodes.)

\paragraph{No single scalar projection uniquely encodes operational priority.} Once $F$ is the primitive, the natural next step is to derive a single scalar to rank by. We show empirically that this is structurally fragile: pairwise stochastic dominance against the teleoperated reference (AUC $= P(T_{\text{model}} < T_{\text{ref}})$) and integrated metrics (restricted-mean survival time, RMST) rank the same four policies in different orders on our data. The cause is not that AUC has an inherent bias; it is that the teleop reference is so much faster than any of the evaluated policies that the AUC integral is dominated by short-time behaviour, where models with sharp early-success peaks win, while integrated metrics weight all of $[0, \tau]$ equally. The takeaway is not ``choose the right scalar'' but ``report the distribution and disclose the aggregation.''

\paragraph{The choice of test, not just the choice of $N$, determines what is resolvable.} Reporting a scalar with confidence intervals is one job; testing whether two policies differ on the underlying distribution is another. We treat them as separate. \S\ref{sec:res-efficiency} shows that on borderline model pairs in our data, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test on the macro CDF reaches 80\% detection at $N{=}20$--$30$ paired rollouts per (model, object) cell where binary success-rate-at-threshold and the integrated RMST scalar do not within budget. The integrator that makes RMST interpretable is the same step that smears the where-do-the-curves-separate information that a sup-norm test exploits. \emph{Scalars are for reporting; tests are for ranking.} Conflating the two wastes statistical power.

\paragraph{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{A canonical primitive.} The time-to-success CDF with hard failures absorbed as $T = \infty$ ghost events. Standard scalars are projections of it; we propose macro-averaged \emph{Human-Relative Throughput} (HRT) as a dimensionless headline scalar grounded in a same-fixture teleoperated reference.
  \item \textbf{Aggregation-rule disagreement as a structural finding.} Same data, principled aggregation rules, opposite rankings. No single scalar uniquely encodes operational priority.
  \item \textbf{Sample-efficiency from the right test, not the right scalar.} KS on the CDF detects ranking differences on borderline pairs at $N{=}20$--$30$ where every binary baseline fails within budget; RMST as a scalar gives essentially no efficiency advantage over $F(60\,\text{s})$. The two-jobs framing -- scalar for reporting, test for ranking -- is the central methodological recommendation.
  \item \textbf{An open benchmark and reference implementation.} PhAIL: four frontier VLAs, four trained objects, $\sim$750 episodes including 372 teleop reference trajectories, full operations logs, synchronized video, and an open-source evaluation framework that implements the methodology end-to-end.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.} The contribution is the comparison primitive, the ranking procedure, the power budget that backs ranking claims, and the open framework that makes new claims auditable. The per-model rankings are illustrative of what the methodology can resolve at a given depth, not architectural pronouncements; we used each model's default published fine-tuning recipe (\S\ref{sec:phail-finetune}). The PhAIL leaderboard is open to submissions (\S\ref{sec:submissions}).

\section{Time-to-Success CDF as Evaluation Primitive}\label{sec:framework}
%===============================================================================

The methodology has three components: the random variable and its CDF (\S\ref{sec:framework-T}), the reporting layer (scalars and visualizations derived from the CDF, \S\ref{sec:framework-reporting}), and the ranking layer (paired tests on the CDF, \S\ref{sec:framework-test}). The strict separation between reporting and ranking is the methodological core.

\subsection{The Random Variable $T$}\label{sec:framework-T}

For a single rollout episode with multiple operations to perform, we extract a sequence of times-to-success $T_i$ and event indicators $E_i \in \{0, 1\}$. A successful placement contributes $T_i$ equal to the elapsed time since the previous successful placement (or since episode start, for the first), with $E_i = 1$. If the episode ends with operations remaining (operator stopped, timed out, model stalled), the unfinished attempt contributes one right-censored pair $(T_{\text{tail}}, 0)$ where $T_{\text{tail}} = \text{duration} - \text{last placement time}$; $E = 0$ means we know $T > T_{\text{tail}}$ but do not observe $T$.

\paragraph{Hard failures as $T = \infty$ ghost events.} Some operations end in states the policy cannot recover from. The clearest case is an item dropped outside the workspace, where the item is physically gone from the scene. Items dropped within the workspace but outside both totes (e.g., on the table) are a softer category: in principle the policy can pick them back up, and we observed at least one rollout where this happened. We count those as failures only if they are still uncollected at episode end: as many failures as there are dropped-and-not-recovered items at the time the rollout terminates. A safety-stop termination is a third class: the rollout ends because the operator (or built-in safety reflexes) stopped it, and the operation that triggered the stop contributes one ghost event. We absorb all of these as $(T = \infty, E = 1)$ ghost events: the operation \emph{did} terminate (so $E = 1$, not censored), but at $T = \infty$ rather than at any finite time. The CDF carries this as an asymptote below $F = 1$; the asymptote's height equals the hard-failure rate. Stalls and timeouts are explicitly \emph{not} ghost events: those are slow-but-recoverable runs that belong in the censored tail, where they correctly indicate ``the policy was working when we stopped watching it.''

\paragraph{Multi-placements.} Occasionally a single grasp deposits several items in one motion (e.g., when a cluster of small batteries comes loose together). Our annotation tooling records each successful placement individually with a timestamp; when annotators marked multiple placements within a fraction of a second, we cluster placements within $\Delta t < 0.3$\,s into one multi-placement event. The first item in the cluster contributes its inter-arrival time $T_i$ to the CDF; subsequent items contribute $T = 0$ with $E = 1$, since they were placed essentially simultaneously. The inter-arrival anchor advances by the cluster's first timestamp only. This is an annotation-pipeline detail rather than a property of the underlying primitive: the tooling could have recorded simultaneous timestamps directly, in which case there would be no $\Delta t$ threshold to set.

The CDF $F(t) = P(T \le t)$ and its complement $S(t) = 1 - F(t)$ are estimated by the Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimator~\cite{kaplan1958} on the $(T, E)$ collection. Confidence intervals on derived scalars (RMST, HRT, $F(\tau)$ at fixed thresholds) are reported via 95\% episode-clustered bootstrap~\cite{efron1993}; pointwise log-log intervals using the Greenwood~\cite{greenwood1926} variance estimator are equivalent and described in~\cite{kalbfleisch2002}. Episodes with zero successes contribute a single right-censored observation of length \texttt{episode\_duration}; we never drop them. The $T = \infty$ atom is handled directly by the estimator (a discrete jump at the right edge); for the integrated-metric calculation in \S\ref{sec:framework-reporting} we set $T_{\text{ghost}} \to \tau$, the integration cap, so a hard failure inflates RMST by exactly $\tau$ minus the time the model would have spent on that operation if successful.

We considered a (per-attempt success probability, per-attempt cycle time) framing and abandoned it. ``Attempt'' is not cleanly defined even for pick-and-place: should an air-grasp count, or only a closed gripper that lifts the object? does a drop-and-retry count as one attempt or two? Two readers can derive different success rates from the same video by drawing the boundary differently. The time-to-success $T$ avoids the question entirely -- the only thing measured is the wall clock until the next success.

\subsection{Reporting: Scalars and Visualizations from the CDF}\label{sec:framework-reporting}

The CDF is the lossless representation. Every standard scalar in published VLA evaluations is a projection of it (Table~\ref{tab:projections}); reporting any single one without the CDF discards information.

\paragraph{Headline scalar: Human-Relative Throughput (HRT).} For each (model, object) cell we compute
\[
  \text{HRT}(m, o) = \frac{\text{RMST}_{\text{ref},\,o}(\tau)}{\text{RMST}_{m, o}(\tau)}, \qquad \tau = 240\,\text{s},
\]
the cell-wise ratio of teleoperated-reference to model RMST, reported as a percentage, and macro-aggregated across objects with equal weights. Three properties make this the right scalar to report. (i) It inherits the CDF's joint speed-and-completion property via $T = \infty$ inflating $\text{RMST}_{\text{model}}$. (ii) It is grounded in operator practice (UPH-equivalent against a same-fixture human reference, so embodiment confounds cancel). (iii) Cell-wise normalization absorbs object-difficulty differences -- a hard object slows teleop and model proportionally -- yielding a dimensionless quantity comparable across heterogeneous tasks. Cross-lab comparison reduces to comparing ratios, not absolute throughputs.

\paragraph{Visualization: P-P plots.} For a (model, object) cell, the P-P plot draws $(F_{\text{model}}(t), F_{\text{ref}}(t))$ parametrically as $t$ sweeps from $0$ to $\tau$. The diagonal is ``as fast as the reference''; below-diagonal is slower; above-diagonal is faster. Two useful geometric facts. The area under the curve equals $P(T_{\text{model}} < T_{\text{ref}})$, the pairwise stochastic-dominance probability (AUC). The maximum vertical distance from the curve to the diagonal equals the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance $D = \sup_t |F_{\text{model}}(t) - F_{\text{ref}}(t)|$ between the two CDFs -- so the P-P plot is the natural visualization for both AUC and KS. P-P plots make distributional shape legible (saturation, early-success peaks, tail behavior); we use them descriptively in \S\ref{sec:res-aggregation}. We do \emph{not} use AUC as a headline scalar: against a much-faster reference it is dominated by what happens at small times where the reference's mass concentrates, which is only one of several legitimate ways to weight a CDF -- and on our data it produces a different ranking than integrated metrics.

\paragraph{Confidence intervals.} All reporting scalars and CDF curves carry 95\% paired episode-clustered bootstrap intervals~\cite{efron1993}. We resample whole episodes (not operations within episodes) because operations within an episode are correlated by the rollout's shared visual conditions and policy state; per-operation bootstrap produces fake-tight intervals.

\subsection{Ranking: Paired Tests on the CDF}\label{sec:framework-test}

Reporting a scalar with a CI is one job; testing whether two policies' underlying time-to-success distributions differ is a different job, and we treat it as such. Our primary test for ranking claims is the two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic~\cite{smirnov1948}
\[
  D = \sup_t \big| F_a(t) - F_b(t) \big|,
\]
computed per-object on the macro-aggregated CDF, with $p$-values from a paired episode-clustered bootstrap~\cite{efron1993} that pools the two policies' subsamples per object under $H_0: F_a = F_b$. As a sanity-check secondary test we report stratified pooled-across-objects logrank~\cite{mantel1966} with Bonferroni correction over the model pairs.

The two-jobs framing -- scalar for reporting, KS for ranking -- is non-trivial. \S\ref{sec:res-efficiency} demonstrates empirically that RMST-as-scalar is not measurably more sample-efficient than $F(60\,\text{s})$ for \emph{ranking}, while KS-as-test materially is. The information lives in \emph{where the curves separate}; an integrator smears that information, a sup-norm test catches it.

\section{PhAIL: Design, Protocol, and Power Budget}\label{sec:phail}
%===============================================================================

\subsection{Platform and Task}\label{sec:phail-platform}

The evaluation station uses a DROID-style configuration~\cite{droid}: a Franka Research 3 with a Robotiq 2F-85 parallel-jaw gripper, plus an over-the-shoulder external RGB camera and a wrist-mounted RGB camera. The first task is bin-to-bin order picking: items move one at a time from an inbound (transparent) tote to an outbound (grey) tote across four object types spanning a useful slice of physical regimes -- wooden spoons (rigid, elongated, multi-grasp), towels (deformable), scissors (articulated, metallic), and batteries (small, rigid). Bin-to-bin is deliberately not the most complex task we could put VLAs through; it is the simplest task with unambiguous success/intervention semantics that still carries clear commercial signal (millions of picks per day across warehousing and fulfilment). The four frontier VLAs we evaluate were trained for substantially more elaborate behaviour than this; the rankings reported below say something specific about how they perform on bin-to-bin under default fine-tuning recipes, not about their architectural ceilings on harder tasks. Subsequent PhAIL releases will add new tasks (including insertion and small-part assembly); the methodology is unchanged across additions. Figure~\ref{fig:platform} shows the platform during a single rollout: synchronized exterior and wrist video, 3D end-effector trajectory, and telemetry traces. Every evaluated rollout is published with these artifacts so any reader can audit a specific time-to-success annotation against the raw video and joint state.

\subsection{Models, Fine-Tuning, and Protocol}\label{sec:phail-finetune}

We evaluate four publicly available VLAs: \textbf{OpenPI $\pi_{0.5}$}~\cite{pi05}, a 3B-parameter VLA with FAST action tokenization atop flow-matching; \textbf{NVIDIA GR00T N1.6}~\cite{groot_n1}, a 3B-parameter VLA combining a Cosmos-Reason VLM with a 32-layer diffusion transformer action head; \textbf{ACT}~\cite{act}, the Action Chunking Transformer trained from scratch, a CVAE that predicts an action chunk; and \textbf{SmolVLA}~\cite{smolvla}, a 450M-parameter VLA from the Hugging Face / LeRobot project. No model demonstrates zero-shot capability on this task, so fine-tuning is a prerequisite. We collected and publish 352 teleoperated demonstrations ($\sim$13 hours of robot operation; Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}). All four models were fine-tuned on the same dataset using each repository's default published recipe; we did not perform per-model hyperparameter search or recipe adaptation. \textbf{This is a deliberate methodological constraint, not best practice for production deployment;} a per-model recipe optimization could shift the rankings of \S\ref{sec:results}.

Each rollout has a time cap of 30\,s per item ($\sim$10$\times$ the $\sim$2.7\,s/item teleop pace). Evaluation is blind: the scheduler randomly selects which model checkpoint runs next and the operator does not know which is running. The operator's only intervention is triggering a safety stop. After each rollout the operator records target/source counts, items lost outside both bins, and outcome label $\in$ \{Success, Stalled, Safety, Ran\_out\_of\_time\}. Every successful placement is annotated post-hoc by a second annotator from the rollout video, yielding the $(T_i, E_i)$ sequence per episode (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\subsection{Cohort and Power Budget}\label{sec:phail-cohort}

After object/model filtering, $\sim$680 episodes enter the analyses; 372 are teleoperated reference rollouts. Annotations come from manual review of rollout video augmented by automated success detection (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}); both label streams are released alongside the data.

How many rollouts does a ranking claim actually need? Two basic calculations, both standard, give the order-of-magnitude. First, if a paper wants to publish a confidence interval of width $\pm 5$ percentage points around a single binary success rate, the Wilson formula needs roughly $N \approx 380$ rollouts per arm. Second, if a paper wants to detect a 5-percentage-point difference between two policies on a paired comparison with 80\% power and $\alpha = 0.05$, the McNemar test needs roughly $N \approx 1280$ paired rollouts per condition (the range 600--1500 in this paper reflects different assumptions about how correlated the paired outcomes are; we use a discordance rate of 0.2 for the central estimate).

Both numbers are far above the field's modal $N = 10$--$20$ per condition, and both reach into the low thousands once the effects to resolve are realistic frontier-VLA gaps. Our own $N \approx 26$ per cell is 2--3$\times$ the field median but still well below either threshold; \S\ref{sec:res-efficiency} translates this analytical picture into empirical detection-rate-vs-$N$ curves on our data, and reaches the same conclusion by a different route.

\subsection{Sample Efficiency: The Right Test, Not the Right Scalar}\label{sec:res-efficiency}

The headline above leans on RMST and pooled logrank. We now ask the operational question: at fixed $N$, which metric most reliably detects a ranking difference? For each pair of inference models and each $N \in \{5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30\}$ per (model, object) cell, we ran 200 outer subsampling trials. Each trial subsamples $N$ episodes per cell with replacement, then runs a 100-rep inner episode-clustered bootstrap to compute a two-sided $p$-value for the difference of each candidate metric (macro-averaged across objects). Detection rate is the fraction of trials with $p < 0.05$. Figure~\ref{fig:hero}b shows the result for the borderline pair GR00T vs.\ ACT; Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency} shows all six pairs.

The table reads in three regimes. \textbf{Three saturated pairs} (any inference model vs.\ SmolVLA): all metrics work; effects are large; binary baselines are slightly faster than KS at saturation because the pooled-resample $H_0$ calibration is conservative when effects are huge. \textbf{Two borderline pairs} (OpenPI vs.\ ACT, GR00T vs.\ ACT) -- the bucket where sample efficiency actually matters: KS reaches 80\% detection within budget; $F(30\,\text{s})$, $F(60\,\text{s})$, and RMST never cross 80\% within $N \le 30$. \textbf{One true tie} (OpenPI vs.\ GR00T): no metric reaches 80\% by $N = 30$; KS climbs to $0.70$ while the others stay near $0.09$. Resolving this pair empirically would require the 600--1500 paired rollouts the analytical power calculation predicts (\S\ref{sec:phail-cohort}).

Detection rate at $N = 30$ averaged across all six pairs: $F(30\,\text{s}) = 0.56$, $F(60\,\text{s}) = 0.65$, RMST $= 0.63$, \textbf{KS $= 0.92$}. The $+27$\,pp gain of KS over $F(60\,\text{s})$ is concentrated entirely on the two borderline pairs.

Two takeaways. First, \textbf{a CDF-level test (KS) is materially more sample-efficient than any single CDF-derived scalar (RMST, $F(60\,\text{s})$) for ranking.} RMST is essentially tied with $F(60\,\text{s})$; the integration over $[0, \tau]$ does not buy ranking power, because the information lives in \emph{where the curves separate} and an integrator smears that information. Second, \textbf{empirical and analytical evidence triangulate.} The closest pair is unresolvable at $N = 30$ by every metric, and the analytical 600--1500-paired-rollout requirement predicts exactly that. The protocol-design recommendation is direct: separate the reporting scalar from the ranking test. Report RMST or HRT as the headline; test rankings with paired KS (or stratified logrank) on the full CDF. Treating a single scalar as both the report and the test wastes statistical power.

\paragraph{What KS does and does not say.} A two-sample KS test reports that two CDFs are different; it does not by itself say which policy is faster. The signed direction comes from a paired scalar -- in our case the macro RMST difference (or, equivalently, the sign of $F_a(t) - F_b(t)$ at any $t$ where the difference is large). In practice we read both: KS provides the existence claim (``these distributions differ at $p < 0.05$''), and the scalar provides the direction (``policy $a$ has lower RMST than $b$''). The two outputs together are what we recommend reporting.

\subsection{Aggregation-Rule Disagreement}\label{sec:res-aggregation}

The headline (Table~\ref{tab:headline}) ranks under integrated RMST. We tested whether a different principled aggregation rule over the same CDF would agree. \textbf{Pairwise stochastic dominance vs.\ teleop}, AUC $= P(T_{\text{model}} < T_{\text{teleop}})$, macro-averaged across objects: ACT $0.067$ [$0.052$, $0.084$] $>$ OpenPI $0.044$ [$0.034$, $0.058$] $\approx$ GR00T $0.041$ [$0.030$, $0.054$] $>$ SmolVLA $0.018$. ACT is now \emph{highest} among inference models. \textbf{Integrated metrics} (RMST, HRT, intervention rate): ACT third, behind GR00T and OpenPI.

The disagreement is not noise; it is structural, and on our data it is sharpened by a property of the teleop reference rather than by ACT alone. The teleop CDF is concentrated on small times -- nearly all teleop attempts finish in under 30\,s -- so the AUC integral $P(T_{\text{model}} < T_{\text{teleop}})$ is dominated by what happens at the short-time end of the model's CDF. A policy with a fat ``fast-when-it-works'' early peak (ACT) wins more of those early-finish races against a tightly-distributed teleop than a steadily-paced one does, even if the steadily-paced policy completes more attempts overall. AUC asks ``which policy reaches its first success first?''; RMST asks ``which policy has the lower mean time-to-next-success including hard failures as $\infty$?''. Both are well-defined; both are implemented in our public code; neither is ``right.''

\paragraph{Recommendation.} We recommend \textbf{HRT as the default headline scalar}, because it inherits the joint reliability-throughput property of the CDF (via $T = \infty$ inflating the denominator) and matches operator-facing UPH practice. AUC against a much-faster reference is not a good co-headline on \emph{this} dataset: as the data above show, when the reference's CDF is concentrated at small times the AUC integral is dominated by short-time behaviour, and ``which model registered an early success'' overrides ``which model completed more attempts overall.'' This is a property of the teleop reference being seven times faster than any evaluated VLA, not a property of AUC itself; on a benchmark where the reference and the policies operated at comparable speeds, AUC and RMST would track much more closely. When the headline scalar disagrees with another principled aggregation, the right move is not to publish both with equal weight but to disclose which operational priority the choice encodes. Cross-model P-P plots (\S\ref{sec:res-pairwise}, without the teleop anchor) are useful as a visualization of distributional shape; they are a byproduct of the CDF, not an independent metric.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%===============================================================================

\subsection{Position Against Prior Benchmarks}

\textbf{Simulated benchmarks} (RoboCasa, CALVIN, ManiSkill, SIMPLER \cite{robocasa,calvin,maniskill,simpler}) are fast to iterate but do not capture real-world perception, latency, or safety. \textbf{Real-hardware research benchmarks} such as RoboArena \cite{roboarena} and RoboChallenge \cite{robochallenge} use physical robots but compare via pairwise Elo or scalar success on household and lab tasks; neither reports throughput or reliability, nor compares distributionally over operations.

\textbf{Competition-style evaluations} (RGMC at ICRA/IROS, OCRTOC~\cite{ocrtoc}, NIST Assembly Task Boards~\cite{nist_atb}, the Real Robot Challenge~\cite{realrobotchallenge}, the Robothon Grand Challenge~\cite{so2023}, surveyed in~\cite{sun_competitions_2022}) all force standardized scoring on a shared task. Closest in spirit is the Digital Robot Judge~\cite{so2023}, which builds task-centric performance databases via electronic task boards -- complementary to our video-and-telemetry release. PhAIL differs in being live between events rather than episodic, anchoring the headline scalar to a teleoperated reference, and reporting time-to-success distributionally rather than as a per-attempt success rate.

\textbf{Methodology critiques.} Kress-Gazit et al.~\cite{kress2024} and the LBM examination~\cite{lbm2026} are existence proofs that good real-robot evaluation is feasible: blinded same-session A/B, hundreds of rollouts. They prescribe; we ship a metric framework with a reference implementation. Agarwal et al.~\cite{agarwal2021} are the closest precedent for distributional comparison in ML evaluation; we extend their approach to time-to-event data with $T = \infty$ ghost events.

\subsection{Why CDF-Level Tests, Not Just CDF-Derived Scalars}

The central new finding of this paper is that \emph{which test you use determines what ranking you can resolve at a given $N$}, and that choosing the right test is a much larger lever than choosing the right scalar. RMST, our headline reporting scalar, is \emph{not} measurably more sample-efficient than $F(60\,\text{s})$ for ranking. The integration step that makes RMST interpretable as ``average time to next success'' is the same step that smears the where-do-the-curves-separate information that a sup-norm test exploits. The result is the central methodological recommendation of the paper: scalars are for reporting, tests are for ranking; conflating them wastes power.

\subsection{Honest Limits}

Single embodiment (Franka FR3 + Robotiq 2F-85, fixed camera layout); generalization to mobile, bimanual, or different end-effectors is future work. Pick-and-place primitive only; the framework applies unchanged to any operation with a teleoperated reference and event-time outcome (insertion, packing, navigation, articulated manipulation), but PhAIL's empirical validation is restricted to bin-to-bin across four trained objects. $N \approx 26$ per cell is below what binary metrics need to rank close pairs (\S\ref{sec:res-efficiency} treats this as a feature, not a bug). The empirical numbers are illustrative of what the methodology can resolve at our depth; per-model recipe optimization could shift them.

\subsection{Recommendations}

The single recommendation that does the most work, on top of everything above, is \textbf{run policies in blind, randomized rotation on the same fixture in the same session.} Operator and environment shift outcomes by margins comparable to or larger than gaps between adjacent models -- the spatial-configuration sensitivity of Appendix~\ref{app:spatial} shows GR00T losing 22\,pp completion when the camera and outbound tote are placed on opposite sides. If the two models being compared are not run interleaved on the same workspace state by the same operator on the same day, what looks like a model-vs-model effect can be an operator-vs-operator or session-vs-session effect. Same-fixture blinded random rotation is a precondition for the rest of the methodology to hold; without it, the CDF you measure is contaminated by the protocol that produced it.

The remaining recommendations follow from the body of the paper: (i) report the time-to-success CDF, not just a binary success rate at a single threshold; (ii) treat hard failures (items lost outside the workspace, items left dropped on the table at episode end, safety-stop terminations) as $T = \infty$ ghost events rather than additional censoring; (iii) ground the headline scalar in a same-fixture teleoperated reference and macro-aggregate cell-wise across tasks; (iv) report scalars with paired episode-clustered bootstrap intervals, and test rankings with paired KS (or stratified logrank) on the full CDF; (v) plan $N$ from the pair you most want to resolve, by plotting detection-rate-vs-$N$ curves on pilot data (as in \S\ref{sec:res-efficiency}) rather than picking a round number.

\subsection{Submissions Welcome}\label{sec:submissions}

The leaderboard at \url{https://phail.ai} is open to submissions. Teams can run their own checkpoints on the published evaluation protocol (or on the PhAIL operator's hardware, on request) and have the resulting CDFs scored by the same framework against the same teleoperated reference. The four rankings reported in this paper reflect each model's default open-source recipe; we expect them to move as the field publishes better recipes, and the framework is built to absorb those updates.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:future}

Four commitments follow from the open release.

\paragraph{API-based submissions.} Teams expose an inference HTTP endpoint that takes the standard observation contract (camera images, end-effector pose, language prompt) and returns actions; PhAIL operators run rollouts on the shared hardware and score the resulting CDFs against the published teleoperated reference. Teams keep control of their weights and infrastructure; the rollout protocol stays consistent. This minimizes the engineering distance between ``we have a VLA'' and ``we have a result on the leaderboard,'' which is the practical meaning of an open benchmark.

\paragraph{More tasks.} Insertion (USB-A, Ethernet) and small-part assembly are the next PhAIL tasks. Both have unambiguous success states and admit a teleoperated reference. The methodology applies unchanged.

\paragraph{More architectures.} As new public VLA releases appear, we evaluate them on the existing PhAIL tasks against the same teleoperated reference and update the leaderboard in place. The methodology is the constant; the rankings move as the field publishes.

\paragraph{Automated success detection.} Manual placement annotation is the most expensive and most bias-prone step in the current pipeline. We are extending the detection pipeline to handle pick-and-place tasks without manual review, and to bootstrap directly on new tasks as they are added. This is what makes per-task evaluation cost tractable as the benchmark grows.

The evaluation framework is open source; teams can reproduce the full protocol in their own labs without coordination from us.

%===============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%===============================================================================

If the field adopts this methodology, three things change. \textbf{First, scalar choice becomes a disclosed methodological commitment, not a default.} A leaderboard that reports HRT discloses an integrated-throughput priority; one that reports AUC-vs-teleop discloses a pairwise-stochastic-dominance priority; the same data can support either ranking, and a paper that picks one without saying so has elided a load-bearing decision. \textbf{Second, ranking claims become falsifiable.} A KS test on the macro CDF with paired episode-clustered bootstrap and a published power calculation lets a reader check whether $N$ was sufficient to support the claim being made; ``no, we cannot rank these models at this $N$'' becomes a publishable finding rather than a discounted footnote. \textbf{Third, the hidden cost of binary success-rate-at-threshold becomes visible:} on borderline policy pairs, the binary metric throws away enough information about the underlying CDF that no realistic $N$ recovers it, while the corresponding CDF-level test resolves the same comparison at a tractable budget. The leaderboard at \url{https://phail.ai} is open to submissions, and we expect the methodology to be most useful as the field publishes better fine-tuning recipes and the rankings move accordingly.

PhAIL's protocol logs the configuration with every episode so that this kind of confound is visible to the analyst rather than absorbed into noise.

\section{Power-Calculation Derivations}\label{app:power}
McNemar derivation for binary paired ranking; $t$-test derivation for RMST paired ranking; KS subsampling-based empirical power curves with full $(N, \text{metric}, \text{detection rate})$ tables.

\section{Annotation Protocol}\label{app:protocol}

The framework requires per-episode event logs (place timestamps, intervention counts, end-state item counts). The current protocol obtains them in two parallel streams; both are released. The protocol is implementation, not contribution, and is expected to evolve.

\paragraph{Crowd annotation.} High-tier crowd annotators view the synchronized exterior and wrist videos at 30\,fps via a custom Toloka task. They record the timestamp of each successful place, the count of items in the source and target totes at end of run, and a count of operator-visible interventions. Annotator instructions cover the three intervention classes (drops outside totes, safety stops, stalls) with edge-case examples drawn from a curated 10-episode pilot.

\paragraph{Autopilot.} A telemetry parser emits per-event labels via three stages: \emph{detect} extracts edges from the gripper command signal; \emph{featurize} attaches per-edge features (hold duration, end-effector displacement, target-zone indicator, prior-open gap, post-close measured grip); \emph{classify} maps the feature dict to a label (pick / place / drop / phantom). The classifier is the only stage that makes label decisions, isolating tunable thresholds in one file.

\paragraph{Cross-validation.} Crowd labels are the gold reference. The autopilot is evaluated against them on every annotated episode; systematic disagreement is reported and used to refine the autopilot rather than override the gold. Both label streams are public.
