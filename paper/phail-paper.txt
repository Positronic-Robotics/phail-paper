                                                         Abstract

1              How do you tell whether one robot policy is better than another in the real world?
2              Most published evaluations of vision-language-action (VLA) policies answer this
3              with binary success rate at a ﬁxed timeout, no conﬁdence intervals, no paired test,
4              N ≤25 per condition – under-budgeted by roughly two orders of magnitude for
5              the ranking claims it publishes. We respond with a distributional methodology
6              for VLA evaluation, and release PhAIL – a real-world inference benchmark that
7              instantiates it. The time-to-success CDF is the evaluation primitive, absorbing
8              hard failures (drops outside the workspace, safety stops) as T =∞ ghost events.
9              Reporting and ranking are separated: a scalar with bootstrap intervals (Human-
10             Relative Throughput, HRT) for reporting; paired Kolmogorov–Smirnov on the
11             CDF for ranking. On a Franka FR3 across four objects with ∼950 episodes
12             and ∼35 paired rollouts per (model, object) cell, we evaluate four frontier VLAs
13             (OpenPI π0.5 , GR00T N1.6, ACT, SmolVLA): the best frontier model is roughly
14             7× slower than a human teleoperator, the top two are statistically indistinguish-
15             able, and SmolVLA is clearly worst. Two structural ﬁndings: different reasonable
16             scalars rank the same models in different orders, and a distributional test resolves
17             the closest ranking with ∼30× fewer trials than binary success rate. Benchmark,
18             dataset, and reference implementation: URL withheld for double-blind review.



19   1 Introduction

20   How do you tell whether one robot policy is better than another in the real world? This question is the
21   gate to every deployment claim in robotics, and the answer the ﬁeld has converged on is structurally
22   insufﬁcient for the claims it makes. Most published evaluations of vision-language-action (VLA)
23   policies report binary success rate at a ﬁxed timeout, with no conﬁdence intervals and no paired
24   test, at N ≤ 25 rollouts per condition. As VLA development accelerates and frontier models begin
25   to compete head-to-head on real hardware, getting evaluation right is what tells the ﬁeld whether
26   progress is real or the rankings are noise.
27   The standard ﬁx the ﬁeld has reached for – adding a throughput metric (UPH, cycle time, comple-
28   tion fraction) alongside binary success – works in part but surfaces a deeper problem: when the two
29   scalars rank policies in different orders, the choice between them is task-dependent and rarely dis-
30   closed in print. Recent methodology critiques argue that blinded same-session A/B with hundreds
31   of rollouts is feasible [19, 20], and the closest ML-side precedent for distributional comparison [21]
32   extends the conversation toward distributions. None of these recommendations ships a primitive
33   plus a reference implementation that lets the ﬁeld apply them on a shared station.
34   We respond by adopting the time-to-success CDF as the evaluation primitive. Let T be the wall-clock
35   time the policy needs to complete one operation; hard failures (items lost outside the workspace,

69    Scope. The methodological contribution is the comparison primitive, the ranking procedure, the
70    power budget, and the open framework that makes new claims auditable. The methodology is task-
71    agnostic – it applies to any operation with an unambiguous success event and a same-ﬁxture human
72    reference. PhAIL’s empirical validation is bin-to-bin pick-and-place across four objects (§4); other
73    natural ﬁts include insertion, packing, navigation, and articulated manipulation. The per-model rank-
74    ings are illustrative of what the methodology can resolve at our depth, not architectural pronounce-
75    ments; we used each repository’s default ﬁne-tuning recipe (§4.2). PhAIL is open to submissions.

76    2 Related Work
77    Existing real-robot evaluation protocols. RoboArena [9] and RoboChallenge [8] use physical
78    robots but compare via pairwise Elo or scalar success on standardized tasks; neither reports con-
79    ﬁdence intervals or paired tests on the rankings. Competition-style evaluations (RGMC, OCR-
80    TOC [15], NIST Assembly Task Boards [16], the Real Robot Challenge [17], surveyed in [14]) stan-
81    dardize task scoring without statistical machinery; closest in spirit is the Digital Robot Judge [18],
82    which builds task-centric performance databases via electronic task boards but reports raw telemetry
83    only. The most rigorous existing real-robot evaluation is the LBM examination [20], which runs
84    blinded same-session A/B at N =50–200 per condition with Bayesian posterior violins on success
85    rate, paired Barnard’s exact tests for binary outcomes, Welch’s t-tests for a scalar task-completion
86    score, and Bonferroni-corrected signiﬁcance grouping – a strong precedent for what rigorous real-
87    robot evaluation looks like. PhAIL’s contribution is orthogonal to LBM’s: a richer primitive (time-to-
88    success CDF with T =∞ ghost events, jointly carrying reliability and throughput) and a same-ﬁxture
89    human-reference anchor that turns embodiment-speciﬁc timings into a dimensionless ratio. Simu-
90    lated benchmarks (RoboCasa, CALVIN, ManiSkill, SIMPLER [10, 11, 12, 13]) iterate fast but miss
91    real-world perception, latency, and safety; they are complementary, not substitutes.

92    Methodology critiques. Kress-Gazit et al. [19] argue that current real-robot evaluation is statisti-
93    cally underpowered and recommend blinded same-session A/B at hundreds of rollouts; STEP [23]
94    concurs (“20–30 real-world trials are insufﬁcient for statistically signiﬁcant conclusions”). On the
95    ML side, Agarwal et al. [21] are the closest precedent for distributional comparison in evaluation.
96    We extend their distributional approach to time-to-event data with T =∞ ghost events and ship a
97    reference implementation of Kress-Gazit’s recommendations on a shared station. A 13-paper survey
98    of recent VLA evaluation practice (Appendix A) conﬁrms the same pattern: modal per-condition
99    N is 10–20, and none of the 13 standard-practice papers report conﬁdence intervals or paired tests;
100   LBM is the single recent counter-example.

101   3 Time-to-Success CDF as Evaluation Primitive
102   The methodology has three components: the random variable and its CDF (§3.1), the reporting layer
103   (scalars and visualizations derived from the CDF, §3.2), and the ranking layer (paired tests on the
104   CDF, §3.3). The strict separation between reporting and ranking is the methodological core. We
105   use concrete language drawn from our running bin-to-bin pick-and-place setup (formal description
106   in §4) – successful placements, items dropped outside the workspace – but the framework applies
107   unchanged to any operation with an unambiguous success event, a measurable time-to-success, an
108   identiﬁable notion of hard failure (states from which the policy cannot recover), and a same-ﬁxture
109   human-reference baseline.

110   3.1   The Random Variable T

111   For a single rollout episode with multiple operations to perform, we extract a sequence of times-
112   to-success Ti and event indicators Ei ∈ {0, 1}. A successful placement contributes Ti equal
113   to the elapsed time since the previous successful placement (or since episode start, for the ﬁrst),
114   with Ei = 1. If the episode ends with operations remaining (operator stopped, timed out,


                                                        3
115   model stalled), the unﬁnished attempt contributes one right-censored pair (Ttail , 0) where Ttail =
116   duration − last placement time; E = 0 means we know T > Ttail but do not observe T .

117   Hard failures as T = ∞ ghost events. Some operations end in states the policy cannot recover
118   from. The clearest case is an item dropped outside the workspace, where the item is physically gone
119   from the scene. Items dropped within the workspace but outside both totes (e.g., on the table)
120   are a softer category: in principle the policy can pick them back up, and we observed at least
121   one rollout where this happened. We count those as failures only if they are still uncollected at
122   episode end: as many failures as there are dropped-and-not-recovered items at the time the rollout
123   terminates. A safety-stop termination is a third class: the rollout ends because the operator stopped
124   it, and the operation that triggered the stop contributes one ghost event. We absorb all of these as
125   (T = ∞, E = 1) ghost events: the operation did terminate (so E = 1, not censored), but at T = ∞
126   rather than at any ﬁnite time. The CDF carries this as an asymptote below F = 1; the asymptote’s
127   height equals the hard-failure rate. Stalls and timeouts are explicitly not ghost events: those are
128   slow-but-recoverable runs that belong in the censored tail, where they correctly indicate “the policy
129   was working when we stopped watching it.”

130   Estimation and censoring. The CDF F (t) = P (T ≤ t) is estimated by the Kaplan-Meier
131   product-limit estimator [1] on the (T, E) collection. Conﬁdence intervals on derived scalars (RMST,
132   HRT, F (τ ) at ﬁxed thresholds) are reported via 95% episode-clustered bootstrap [4]. Episodes with
133   zero successes contribute a single right-censored observation of length τepisode , the per-rollout time
134   budget after which an unﬁnished operation is censored (set by the protocol; §4.2); we never drop
135   them. The T = ∞ atom is handled directly by the estimator (a discrete jump at the right edge);
136   for the integrated-metric calculation in §3.2 we set Tghost → τ , the integration cap, so a hard fail-
137   ure inﬂates RMST by exactly τ minus the time the model would have spent on that operation if
138   successful.

165   3.3   Ranking: Paired Tests on the CDF

166   Reporting a scalar with a CI is one job; deciding whether two policies’ underlying distributions
167   differ – and if so, in what direction – is a different job. We treat it as such. By ranking we mean
168   the joint claim of signiﬁcance plus direction, with three possible outcomes: (i) A is better than
169   B (CDFs separate, consistently signed – one dominates the other across t); (ii) A and B differ
170   but neither is uniformly better (CDFs cross – reasonable scalars give opposite signs because they
171   weight different regions of the curve, so disagreement among scalars is the CDF’s signal that the
172   policies have different distributional shapes, not a scalar bug); (iii) A and B are indistinguishable
173   at the available N .
174   Our primary test for the signiﬁcance gate (regimes (i)+(ii) vs (iii)) is the two-sample Kolmogorov–
175   Smirnov statistic [3]

183   The two-jobs framing – scalar for reporting, KS for the signiﬁcance gate – is non-trivial. §5.2

186   curves separate; an integrator smears that information, a sup-norm test catches it.

187   4 PhAIL: Design, Protocol, and Power Budget
188   4.1   Platform and Task

189   The evaluation station uses a DROID-style conﬁguration [7]: a Franka Research 3 with a Robotiq 2F-
190   85 parallel-jaw gripper, plus an over-the-shoulder external RGB camera and a wrist-mounted RGB
191   camera. The ﬁrst task is bin-to-bin order picking: items move one at a time from an inbound (trans-
192   parent) tote to an outbound (grey) tote across four object types spanning a useful slice of physical
193   regimes – wooden spoons (rigid, elongated, multi-grasp), towels (deformable), scissors (articulated,
194   metallic), and batteries (small, rigid). Each rollout episode contains many independent placement
195   operations, which we exploit for sample size; the framework applies just as well to single-operation
196   episodes. Bin-to-bin is deliberately not the most complex task we could put VLAs through; it is the
197   simplest task with unambiguous success/intervention semantics that still carries clear commercial
198   signal (millions of picks per day across warehousing and fulﬁlment). Subsequent PhAIL releases
199   will add new tasks (insertion, small-part assembly); the methodology is unchanged across additions.
200   The same hardware operates the human reference and every evaluated VLA via an open-source eval-
201   uation framework [6] behind a common station interface, so only weights and architecture-speciﬁc
202   observation-to-action code differ across submissions. Every evaluated rollout is published with syn-
203   chronized video, telemetry, automated event labels, and crowd-sourced annotations, browsable in
204   a run-explorer interface (Figure 6, Appendix C; annotation pipeline in Appendix G). Benchmark,
205   dataset, and per-rollout artifacts: URL withheld for double-blind review.

206   4.2   Models, Fine-Tuning, and Protocol

207   We evaluate four publicly available VLAs: OpenPI π0.5 [25] (3B-param, FAST action tokeniza-
208   tion on ﬂow-matching), GR00T N1.6 [26] (3B-param, Cosmos-Reason VLM + 32-layer diffusion
209   transformer action head), ACT [27] (Action Chunking Transformer, CVAE), and SmolVLA [28]
210   (450M-param, HuggingFace/LeRobot). None has zero-shot capability on this task, so ﬁne-tuning is
211   a prerequisite. All four were ﬁne-tuned on the same 352-episode bin-to-bin demonstration set (∼13
212   hours; Appendix D) using each repository’s default recipe – no per-model hyperparameter search.
213   This is a deliberate methodological constraint, not best practice for production deployment;
214   per-model recipe optimization could shift the rankings of §5.
215   Each rollout has a 30 s/item time cap (∼10× the ∼2.7 s/item human pace). Evaluation is blind:
216   the scheduler randomly selects which checkpoint runs next, the operator does not know which is
217   running, and the operator’s only intervention is triggering a safety stop. Post-rollout, the operator
218   records target/source counts, items lost outside both bins, and outcome label ∈ {Success, Stalled,
219   Safety, Ran_out_of_time}; every successful placement is annotated post-hoc from the rollout video
220   (Appendix G).

221   4.3   Cohort and Power Budget

222   After object/model ﬁltering, ∼950 episodes enter the analyses; 386 are human reference rollouts.
223   Annotations come from manual review of rollout video augmented by automated success detection
224   (Appendix G); both label streams are released alongside the data.
225   How many rollouts does a ranking claim actually need? Standard power calculations (Appendix F)
226   put the ﬁeld-modal N =10–20 (Appendix A) orders of magnitude under-budget: a ±5 pp Wilson
227   CI on a single arm needs N ≈380, and detecting a 5 pp paired difference between two policies
228   (McNemar, 80% power, α=0.05) needs 600–1500 paired rollouts. Our own N ≈ 35 per cell is
229   2–3× the ﬁeld median but still well below either threshold; §5.2 conﬁrms this empirically with
230   detection-rate-vs-N curves on our data.

282   5.3   Aggregation-Rule Disagreement

283   Both integrated RMST and AUC vs. human reference are principled scalars derived from the
284   same CDF; the choice of which to extract is itself a methodological commitment. Macro-AUC
285   across objects: ACT 0.138 [0.111, 0.167] > OpenPI 0.101 [0.084, 0.118] ≈ GR00T 0.096 [0.080,
286   0.112] > SmolVLA 0.027 [0.017, 0.038]. ACT is now highest among inference models; under inte-
287   grated RMST/HRT it sits third behind GR00T and OpenPI. Same data, two principled aggregations,
288   opposite top-1s.
289   This is outcome (ii) of §3.3: ACT and OpenPI/GR00T are different (KS rejects) but neither is
290   uniformly better – their CDFs cross, with ACT winning the early-time region (high AUC) and
291   OpenPI/GR00T winning the integrated region (low RMST). The disagreement is sharpened by the
292   human reference being ∼7× faster than any evaluated VLA, collapsing the AUC integral onto the
293   short-time region where ACT’s early-success peak wins more ﬁrst-success races. When CDFs cross,
294   no single scalar can summarize the comparison without weighting one region over another.
295   We recommend HRT as the default headline scalar – it inherits the CDF’s joint reliability-throughput
296   property and matches operator-facing UPH practice; on a benchmark where reference and policies
297   operated at comparable speeds, AUC-vs-human would track RMST closely. Pairwise model-vs-
298   model AUC (Figure 5, Appendix B) conﬁrms the RMST-side conclusion that the top three are indis-
299   tinguishable; per-(model, object) RMST (Figure 4, Appendix B) shows the GR00T-vs-ACT object
300   crossing visible at our N but not clearing Bonferroni at α = 0.05/24.


301   6 Discussion and Conclusion

302   We introduced PhAIL, an open real-robot benchmark and distributional evaluation methodology
303   for vision-language-action policies. The methodology adopts the time-to-success CDF as primitive,
304   separates reporting (Human-Relative Throughput) from ranking (paired Kolmogorov–Smirnov on
305   the macro CDF), and anchors to a same-ﬁxture human reference. Evaluating four frontier VLAs
306   across four objects, two structural ﬁndings emerged: principled aggregation rules over the same
307   CDF yield opposite top-1 rankings, and paired KS detects ranking differences roughly 30× more
308   sample-efﬁciently than binary success-rate baselines.

309   Limitations. Single embodiment (Franka FR3, ﬁxed cameras), single primitive (bin-to-bin pick-
310   and-place, four trained objects); the framework generalizes to any operation with a human reference
311   and event-time outcome but the empirical validation does not. N ≈35 per cell is 2–3× the ﬁeld
312   median but still below what binary metrics need to rank close pairs (§5.2); per-model rankings are
313   illustrative. The single protocol recommendation that does the most work is blind, randomized
314   rotation on the same ﬁxture in the same session – spatial-conﬁguration shifts (Appendix E) move
315   outcomes by margins comparable to gaps between adjacent models, contaminating the CDF if not
316   controlled.
317   If the ﬁeld adopts this methodology, three things change. Scalar choice becomes a disclosed com-
318   mitment (HRT and AUC-vs-human encode different priorities). Ranking claims become falsiﬁ-
319   able via paired KS: “we cannot rank these at this N ” becomes a publishable ﬁnding. The hidden
320   cost of binary thresholds becomes visible: on close pairs, no realistic N recovers what a CDF-level
321   test resolves within budget. PhAIL (URL withheld for double-blind review) is open to submissions;
322   insertion and small-part assembly are next.

435   F    Power-Calculation Derivations
436   Wilson conﬁdence interval on a single binary success rate. For an observed      p proportion p̂ on N
437   trials, the Wilson score interval gives a CI of half-width approximately zα/2 p̂(1 − p̂)/N for the
438   underlying success probability. To obtain ±5 pp at 95% conﬁdence on p̂ = 0.70, the formula gives
439   N ≈ 380 rollouts. At the ﬁeld-modal N ∈ [10, 20], the Wilson 95% CI on an observed 70% success
440   rate is roughly [0.40, 0.89] at N =10 or [0.48, 0.86] at N =20 – intervals so wide that ranking claims
441   at this depth are not statistically defensible.

442   McNemar test for paired binary outcomes. For two policies evaluated on the same N paired
443   conditions, let b and c be the discordant cell counts (one policy succeeds, the other fails). The

445   detect a ∆ = 5 pp paired difference at 80% power and α = 0.05, with discordance rate pd ∈
446   [0.10, 0.25] (capturing “how often the two policies disagree on a given paired condition”),

454   G Annotation Protocol
455   The framework requires per-episode event logs (place timestamps, intervention counts, end-state
456   item counts). The current protocol obtains them in two parallel streams; both are released. The
457   protocol is implementation, not contribution, and is expected to evolve.

458   Crowd annotation. High-tier crowd annotators view the synchronized exterior and wrist videos
459   at 30 fps via a custom Toloka task. They record the timestamp of each successful place, the count
460   of items in the source and target totes at end of run, and a count of operator-visible interventions.
461   Annotator instructions cover the three intervention classes (drops outside totes, safety stops, stalls)
462   with edge-case examples drawn from a curated 10-episode pilot.

463   Autopilot. A telemetry parser emits per-event labels via three stages: detect extracts edges from
464   the gripper command signal; featurize attaches per-edge features (hold duration, end-effector dis-
465   placement, target-zone indicator, prior-open gap, post-close measured grip); classify maps the fea-
466   ture dict to a label (pick / place / drop / phantom). The classiﬁer is the only stage that makes label
467   decisions, isolating tunable thresholds in one ﬁle.


                                                        15
468   Cross-validation. Crowd labels are the gold reference. The autopilot is evaluated against them on
469   every annotated episode; systematic disagreement is reported and used to reﬁne the autopilot rather
470   than override the gold. Both label streams are public.
